# Research: keeping an inventory of a network one is on but does not own

**Status:** completed on 2026-11-23 as brief 37, and **Partial** in a way that matters. Almost every host that holds a primary text (the EU's law site, the Court of Justice's site, the UK statute site, the Information Commissioner's site, the Nmap site, the unofficial GDPR sites) did not resolve from this session. One page was opened and read: the ZMap best-practices page on GitHub. Everything else rests on web-search summaries, and each claim below is tagged **[read]** or **[search]**. Treat each [search] claim as a lead to check against the primary text, not as a fact. Nothing was installed or run. This is **not legal advice**. The note says what the sources say and where they disagree. It does not say what the law is in any place.

Feeds the retention rule of [decision 0226](../decisions/0226-whether-and-how-long-findings-about-a-network-are-kept.md), which asks for exactly this research as its "revisit if", and the sections it names: [217, Traces, evidence, and repair](../spec/2-kernel/217-traces-evidence-and-repair.md) and [218, Discovery and findings](../spec/2-kernel/218-discovery-and-findings.md). Builds on [note 28](28-how-a-network-can-be-seen-from-one-machine.md) (how a network can be seen) and [note 29](29-network-tools-for-agents-and-the-conduct-of-looking.md) (the conduct norms, whose legal statements were also from summaries only). ## Question

A person runs the product on a machine that is on a network they do not own: a shared office, a hotel, a café, a home with other people's devices. The product may list the devices and services it sees there. Three things are asked.

1. Is such a list personal data about other people, and what does keeping it ask of the person?
2. What do computer-misuse law, data-protection law and the network's own terms say about passively observing versus actively probing?
3. What do established tools do by default?

## Answer in short

- **A device list is very likely personal data about third parties, and the usual ways of softening it do not remove that.** The GDPR's Recital 30 names IP addresses as online identifiers [search]. The UK regulator lists MAC addresses as an example of an online identifier that may be personal data [search]. The EU Court held in *Breyer* that a dynamic IP address is personal data for a site operator who has legal means to identify the visitor, a test that depends on the facts [search]. A Dutch regulator fined a city for Wi-Fi tracking in which the MAC addresses were pseudonymised, because they could be combined with other data [search]. Hostnames can carry a person's name (note 29, part 7).
- **The sources disagree on how far that goes.** *Breyer* is conditional and was decided on the older directive. A Danish authority accepted MAC addresses hashed with a key that changes every 24 hours, in a narrow case [search]. California's rules count an address as personal information only if it can reasonably be linked to a consumer or household, and an example in the state's rules says an unlinked address may fall outside [search]. The safe reading for a design is that an address, a hardware address, and an announced name each count, and that hashing them does not help.
- **Passive and active are not drawn the same way by law and by terms.** Computer-misuse laws turn on authorisation and, in the EU directive, on infringing a security measure [search]. None of the sources found says that reading what a machine already holds, or listening to announcements sent to a whole link, is access to someone else's system. Port scanning is unsettled, and the sources differ by country. Capturing other people's traffic is a different class again: a US appeal court held that the content of an unencrypted Wi-Fi network is not "readily accessible to the general public" under the wiretap statute [search].
- **Shared-network terms are plainer than the law.** Every guest-network policy found that mentions the matter bans port scanning or probing, and many ban monitoring traffic [search]. University policies require written approval from a named security office [search]. A breach of terms is a contract matter, and the US federal prosecutors' own policy says charges will not rest on a terms breach alone [search].
- **Established tools do not persist by default and do not hide.** Nmap on a local Ethernet link uses ARP, and an ordinary user gets TCP connects to ports 80 and 443 [search]. `arp-scan` defaults to 256,000 bits per second and warns that a higher rate can cause an ARP storm [search]. ZMap's practices say to identify yourself, scan no more than needed, offer opt-out, and coordinate locally [read]. None of the sources read states a default for how long findings are kept.
- **What the sources do not answer:** who is the "controller" when a program on a person's machine, which sends nothing to its maker, lists other people's devices; and whether the household exemption covers a person doing this on a home network that has guests. No source found speaks to either. See "What could not be verified".

## 1. Is the list personal data?

**The definitions.**
- GDPR Recital 30 says people may be associated with online identifiers provided by their devices, applications, tools and protocols, such as IP addresses and cookie identifiers, and that these can leave traces which, combined with unique identifiers and other information, may be used to create profiles and identify people [search; the recital page was not opened].
- The UK regulator's guidance names MAC addresses, advertising IDs, account handles and device fingerprints as examples of online identifiers that may be personal data. The page carries a notice that it is under review because of the Data (Use and Access) Act [search].
- *Breyer* (C-582/14, 2016): a dynamic IP address is personal data for a website operator if the operator has the legal means to identify the visitor. The test is whether combining it with the provider's data is a means reasonably likely to be used. A summary says the Court excluded cases where identification is prohibited by law or practically impossible [search]. The judgment concerned the 1995 directive and website operators [search].
- California: personal information is information that identifies, relates to, describes, or could reasonably be linked with a particular consumer or household. The state's rules give the example of an address collected but not linked to anyone [search; the current statute text was not opened].

**Hashing and pseudonyms.**
- The Dutch regulator fined the municipality of Enschede 600,000 euros for Wi-Fi tracking. The addresses were pseudonymised and the regulator held that was not anonymous, because they could be combined with other data. An appeal was reported and its result was not found [search].
- Spanish authorities' joint guidance treats Wi-Fi tracking as personal data and names the MAC address as an identifier in unencrypted frames [search].
- *Disagreement.* The Danish Business Authority held in 2015 that the ePrivacy consent rule did not apply to a traffic counter that hashed MAC addresses with an algorithm changed every 24 hours. Commentary says the decision is narrow and may not apply to longer periods or other purposes [search].

**Why the sources point one way for a device list.** A list of a network's devices holds addresses, hardware addresses, announced names, and which services each runs. Note 29 (part 7) found names that carry the owner's identity and studies in which a device was followed across randomised addresses by joining records. One more fact bears on how long a list stays linkable. Apple's private Wi-Fi address is by default "fixed" per network on secure networks and "rotating" on open and weaker ones. Android 10 and later use a per-network random address by default, which is persistent unless the network profile calls for re-randomising [search]. So a hardware address seen on a network is stable enough to follow a device across visits to that same network, but not across networks. A list kept for a network can therefore track a visitor over time on that network, which is the case the regulators acted on. No source found says whether a list of a network's devices has been judged by any regulator. The reasoning here is the note's, not a source's.

## 2. Who bears the duties, and which duties

**Household exemption.** GDPR Article 2(2)(c) leaves out processing by a person in a purely personal or household activity. The Court read the older equivalent narrowly in *Ryneš* (C-212/13, 2014): a home camera that also covers a public space is outside it [search]. The Irish regulator applies the same line to cameras today [search]. A 2025 law-firm article argues the reading has been stretched, because the text looks at the private purpose and not at the place [search]. What no source says: whether a person listing devices on their own home network, with family and guests on it, is inside the exemption. A person doing it for an employer or a client is working, not at home, on any reading. A shared office or a client's building is in that case.

**If the regulation applies.** What follows is from the regulation as summarised by searches, and from the lead's memory of its articles; the article texts were not opened.
- *Lawful basis.* Legitimate interest (Article 6(1)(f)) needs three cumulative steps: a lawful interest, necessity, and a balance against the people's rights. The EDPB guidelines say that if a less intrusive way to reach the aim exists, the basis cannot be used [search]. The point for the design: a list kept past the run is hard to call necessary when the run's own result already served the purpose.
- *Telling the people.* Article 14 duties apply when data are not obtained from the person. Article 14(5)(b) excuses the notice where it is impossible or would take disproportionate effort. The Polish authority fined a company for relying on that where it had contact details and did not use them, and accepted it where the company had no contact details and would have to search for them [search]. A person on a café network has no way to notify the other customers; the exemption would likely be the argument. This note found no case on a device list.
- *Storage limitation and minimisation* (Article 5(1)(c) and (e)) [memory]: data kept no longer, and no more, than the purpose needs.
- *Protection by design and by default* (Article 25) [memory; one search returned nothing on it].
- *ePrivacy Article 5(3).* This asks for consent before gaining access to information stored in a user's terminal equipment. The EDPB's 2024 guidelines on its technical scope exist [search], and their detail was not read. Whether a probe that reads a device's answer (a service banner, a certificate, a name) is "gaining access" under it is not settled by anything found. It is a question for the design to leave closed by not probing more than the person's purpose needs.

**The product as a party.** The design sends nothing to its maker ("nothing phones home", AGENTS.md). Whether a program that runs wholly on a person's machine and stores data only there makes its maker a controller was not answered by any source. It is a point to put to a lawyer, not one to decide here.

## 3. Passive versus active under computer-misuse and wiretap law

**What the sources say, country by country.** All [search].

- *United States, federal.* The Computer Fraud and Abuse Act turns on access "without authorization" or exceeding it. *Van Buren* (2021) held, in a "gates-up-or-down" reading, that the clause reaches areas of a computer that are off limits to the person, not misuse of what the person may already reach. The Justice Department's 2022 policy tells prosecutors to decline cases of good-faith security research and says charges will not rest on a terms-of-service breach alone. It binds only federal prosecutors and does not cover states or civil suits. Law-firm notes disagree on whether the policy follows *Van Buren*.
- *United States, port scans.* The Nmap project's legal chapter (seen through search) says no federal law explicitly bans port scanning, that many state laws could arguably reach even a ping, and that it advises written authorisation. *Moulton v. VC3* (2000) held a scan did not breach the federal act or the state act in that case; the chapter says the result might differ had the machine crashed. Press coverage called the case "port scans are legal"; other coverage says that overstates a narrow ruling.
- *United States, capture.* *Joffe v. Google* (9th Circuit, 2013): the payload data of an unencrypted Wi-Fi network is not "readily accessible to the general public", so the wiretap statute's exception did not cover Google's collection. The court reasoned from limited geographic reach and the expertise needed to decode. A commentary reads it as limited to unencrypted payload data.
- *United Kingdom.* Part 1, s. 1, of the Computer Misuse Act 1990 makes unauthorised access an offence. Commentary says there is no statutory defence for research, and that the prosecution guidance gives less certainty than the US one. Rapid7 says port scanning could meet the description of the offence, and that the prosecutors do not appear to pursue good-faith research [search]. The CPS text on scanning was not read.
- *European Union.* Directive 2013/40/EU, Article 3, requires criminal law for intentional access "without right" where it infringes a security measure, for cases not minor. "Without right" means not authorised by the owner or another right holder or not permitted under national law (Article 2). A recital says there is no liability where criminal intent is missing, such as mandated testing. The recital number was not confirmed.
- *Germany.* The Federal Constitutional Court held that the tools offence in §202c StGB does not reach dual-use tools and needs at least conditional intent. A practitioner summary says merely using a port scan is not punishable and intent decides.
- *Finland.* Note 29 reported a conviction for scanning a bank's ports by summary. A search for it here found nothing. It stays unverified.

**What this gives for passive versus active.**
- Reading what the machine already holds (its own address, routes, neighbour table) is access to the person's own machine. No source discusses it as an issue.
- Listening for announcements that devices send to a whole link: no source in this survey treats it either way. The *Joffe* reasoning is about message content, which announcements are not, but the survey found no ruling on announcements, and the note does not claim one.
- Asking one device a single ordinary question (a connect, a handshake) is the act the norms in notes 28 and 29 call ordinary client behaviour. The sources disagree on where the scan begins: the law summaries turn on authorisation and intent, not on count of packets.
- A scan over many hosts or ports, any exploit or credential guess, and any capture of traffic are the acts every source treats as needing permission.
- The sources do not agree and no single line is safe in all places. The authorisation question is answered on a shared network by the operator's terms (part 4), not by the person's statement.

## 4. Terms on shared networks

All [search]; the pages were seen as search snippets.

- Guest-network terms from a hospitality-style provider, a city, a plug-in-service vendor, and a UK college ban probing, port scanning or network scanning of other devices. A city's terms ban port scans and other information gathering as a precursor to penetration. A hotel group's terms ban monitoring data or traffic on any network or system without the owner's express authorisation. A university guest-network term bans port mapping and vulnerability scanning and says use may be monitored.
- No hotel or airport terms were found that name probing of other guests' devices. A law-firm blog says hotel terms commonly cover unauthorised access and interference, and some forbid monitoring other users.
- Campus policies at several universities require prior written approval from a named security office before network mapping, port scanning or vulnerability scanning of systems one does not own or administer. One calls port scanning a banned protocol for the risk of interference.
- Terms do not mention reading one's own machine's tables or listening for announcements. A ban on "network mapping" in a campus policy could be read to cover an inventory made by any means. That reading was not tested against any policy text.
- The effect of a breach is loss of access and discipline [search]. The US federal policy above treats a terms breach alone as no ground for a charge. Other countries were not looked at.

## 5. What established tools do by default

- **Nmap.** On a local Ethernet link with enough privilege, host discovery uses ARP. An unprivileged user gets a connect-style probe of ports 80 and 443. A privileged scan of non-local targets sends an echo request, a SYN to 443, an ACK to 80 and a timestamp request. A port scan follows only for hosts found up [search]. The reference guide marks some scripts as intrusive and not for networks without permission (note 29, from the repository).
- **arp-scan.** Default 256,000 bits per second; the man page warns a higher rate may cause an ARP storm; every host on the segment answers ARP even behind a filter [search].
- **ZMap** [read]. Six practices: scan no larger, no more often and no faster than needed; make the team identifiable by DNS entries, WHOIS and a web page; give an opt-out and publish the source ranges; test on your own systems first; coordinate with local IT; disclose results.
- **Wireshark.** No documentation text found tells users not to capture other people's traffic. Community answers say use on one's own network is almost certainly lawful and that capture for other purposes may break wiretap law. These are forum posts and a vendor guide, not authorities [search].
- **Retention.** None of the sources states how long Nmap, `arp-scan`, ZMap or Wireshark keep results. They write to the files and output the user chooses, so the user decides. Home-network and inventory apps were not read; note 29 found only one product (NetAlertX) that states local-only keeping.

## 6. Does naming a network as one's own change anything?

- For the other people on it, nothing found suggests it does. A hardware address on a home network belongs to a guest as much as to a stranger on a café network. The naming changes who may authorise looking, not whose data the list holds.
- For the network's terms and the authorisation question, it changes a great deal, but only if the claim is true. Note 28 found that no signal identifies a network stably and without spoofing, and note 29 treated the person's entitlement as the person's statement. The product cannot check ownership.
- The household exemption (part 2) is the one place where naming might matter in law, and the sources do not decide it.
- Sources for a home network with a landlord, a flatmate or an employer's router were not found.

## What this changes in the design

These are **proposals**. The lead applies or rejects them. Decision 0226 is an owner decision, so a change to it is a new record with status Proposed.

1. **Decision 0226, a new Proposed record.** Add these sentences. "Findings about a network the person has not named are held in memory for the life of the run and are never written to the store or to a trace. This covers every address, hardware address, announced name, and service banner, hashed or not." "A hashed or truncated device identifier is treated as the identifier it stands for." Reason: the sources treat pseudonymised MAC addresses as personal data (Enschede), and the one contrary decision (Denmark) was narrow.
2. **Decision 0226, named-network case.** Replace "kept under the ordinary trace retention" with: "Findings about a named network are kept as a separate retention class, shorter than the trace default of 30 days unless the person sets a longer one, and the person can delete them at any time." Add: "Announced device names and hostnames are not kept in findings by default; the stored finding keeps the address, the kind of service, and the time." Reason: names carry identity (note 29, part 7) and a stable per-network hardware address lets a kept list follow a device across visits (part 1). Mark as an option for the owner: "keep nothing for any network" stays the fallback.
3. **Section 217, the ledger.** Add: "A probe sent to a host on a network the person has not named is recorded by its class, count, time, and network label. The destination addresses are held for the life of the run only." Add to the lead's notes: this conflicts with note 29's "keep the record the field does not keep", since the audit entries are kept one year, and a per-address record of probes is itself a device list. The ledger rule that every write is recorded first applies to writes, not to reads, but probes are a third class in note 29 and the design has not said where they are recorded. The owner should decide the trade-off; this note does not.
4. **Section 218, discovery.** Add: "The scout tells the person, before the first probe on a network that is not named, that shared networks commonly forbid scanning and that the person's statement does not replace the operator's permission. The step is shown once per network." Reason: every guest-network and campus term found bans scanning or mapping without approval, and the product cannot read them.
5. **Section 402 and the ladder in note 28.** Add: "On a network that is not named, the ladder stops at reading the machine's own state and listening for announcements. Directed single requests to a found host, and anything broader, need the person to name the network first." Reason: this is the line the sources draw most consistently (part 3). This sets a design choice, not a legal conclusion.
6. **Section 502 and rule 4.** Add: "Device names, hostnames, and addresses of other people's devices are registered with the redactor as personal data and do not appear in logs, traces, or error messages on a network that is not named." Reason: no product read by note 29 redacts them.
7. **SCOPE.md.** Add under what the product does not do: "It does not keep a list of the devices on a network the person has not named. Kind: choice. Reason: such a list is personal data about other people, and keeping it past the run is not needed for the purpose." Add: "It does not capture other people's traffic. Kind: choice, and the sources treat capture as a separate legal class."
8. **Research brief 37 and the README.** Mark brief 37 answered by this note, with the status Partial and the reason (no primary text opened). Add a new open brief: "the position of a program that runs only on a person's machine under GDPR as controller or processor, and the household exemption for a home network with guests", which needs a lawyer's reading, not a search.
9. **Not proposed.** No sentence here says the product is lawful or unlawful anywhere. If the owner wants a statement for users, it should say that the sources disagree and point to their local rules.
