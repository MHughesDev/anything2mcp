# Research: network tools for agents, and the conduct of looking at a network

**Status:** completed on 2026-11-18, and **Partial** in a way that matters. Many hosts were unreachable from the research session (see "How this was surveyed"), so a good part of Part B rests on search-result summaries and not on the primary texts. Part A rests mostly on the projects' own repository and registry pages, which were read. Nothing was installed or run. Every star, fork, commit and version figure is **as the page rendered it on 2026-11-18** and was not cross-checked. This is **not legal advice**; laws vary by place and by year. Feeds [decision 0222](../decisions/0222-the-product-is-aware-of-the-network-its-machine-is-on.md) and the design rounds under it, and will touch [section 106](../spec/1-product/106-what-it-latches-onto.md), [section 218](../spec/2-kernel/218-discovery-and-findings.md), [section 402](../spec/4-building-tools/402-the-scout-and-who-does-what.md) and [section 502](../spec/5-security/502-defending-against-a-manipulated-agent.md) when a design is accepted. Extends [note 27](27-the-public-landscape-of-agent-tools.md), which surveyed the wider field of agent tools and did not look at networks.

## Question

The owner wants coding agents to be able to inspect the network their machine is on, through tools the product carries, and wants the scout to find what is on that network. Two things must be known before that is designed. What already exists, and what is it like? And what conduct are such tools held to, by the people who run networks, by the norms of the people who scan, and by the law?

## Answer in short

- **Agent tools for networks already exist in quantity, and most set no limit on where they look.** The nmap, port-scan and security-suite wrappers read here take a target (and in one case free-form flags) chosen by the model, and state no allow-list, range cap or rate limit. Two small ones set a cap on the number of targets (256) or the duration (600 seconds). Several say "you need authorization" in the README and enforce nothing.
- **The only surveyed agent tool with a fail-closed scope** is a bug-bounty suite: scanning tools reject every target until the operator lists allowed domains or ranges in configuration, private ranges need a second opt-in, a block list wins over the allow list, and redirects are re-checked. The scope is set by the operator's configuration, not by the model. It is a 44-star project; the pattern is the finding, not its use.
- **Read-only is the common safety control for tools that manage a network's equipment**, and it is almost always a switch the same configuration controls, default off (Zabbix, LibreNMS, Juniper's block lists). Two are better: the Pi-hole server removes write tools from the list and rejects them if called, and the UniFi servers use preview-then-confirm for changes. The AWS network server states that all its tools only describe, get and list.
- **Nothing surveyed that is aimed at agents states a default speed, a self-identification, or a log of what it sent.** The established scanners do better in their documents: ZMap's best practices say to scan at the slowest speed that serves the goal, identify the scanner (reverse DNS, WHOIS, a web page), publish how to opt out, and tell the local IT team. Masscan defaults to 100 packets per second and warns that fast scans can harm networks. Nmap's default is a middle timing template, and it separates "safe" from "intrusive" scripts.
- **The default of a careful inventory is to ask what the network already knows before probing.** NetAlertX imports from the router, the DHCP leases and Pi-hole as well as scanning with arp-scan, and keeps everything local. NetBox is an inventory that, by its own README, does not touch the nodes. Local-link discovery (ARP, mDNS) does not leave the segment.
- **Agent hosts treat the network as deny by default and ask by destination.** The sandbox runtime read here allows nothing unless listed, checks denials first, can ask the person for an unlisted destination, and drops loopback, link-local, multicast and cloud-metadata addresses after resolving the name once. It does **not** deny private-use ranges by default, and says why. Codex and Claude Code, from search summaries, also start with the network off or ask when a new domain is needed.
- **What an address-taking tool is expected to refuse** is stated by the web-security guidance: prefer a fixed allow-list; normalize and then block loopback, private, link-local, unique-local and multicast ranges (including IPv4-mapped IPv6); block cloud-metadata addresses; resolve once, check every address returned, and connect to the one checked (DNS rebinding); do not follow redirects unchecked; accept a host, not a URL. The reference fetch server and one MCP-server scanner already do most of this and state their remaining gaps.
- **The line between looking and hostile conduct is not drawn the same way everywhere.** Published summaries say no US federal law names port scanning as a crime, yet unauthorized-access laws, state laws, providers' and campuses' acceptable-use rules, and other countries' laws can reach it. Campus policies read here ban scanning systems one does not own without written approval from a named office. For a tool run by a person on their own network the clear case is a network the person owns or administers.
- **The norms that make a scanner trusted are the same few things in every source:** say who you are, be slow, stay inside what you were given, let the owner opt out, keep a record, never try credentials, never exploit. Nmap's own script categories show the line: some default scripts are "intrusive", and the guide says they should not be run on networks without permission.
- **A list of devices on a home or office network is personal data.** Studies found device names that include people's names, MAC addresses and UUIDs in plain text in local traffic, and a randomized MAC address did not stop tracking when a stable hostname was visible. The one product read here that states its privacy stance (NetAlertX) keeps data local. No product read here redacts device names in logs.
- **No write-up was found of an agent actually being steered into scanning an internal range.** The published concern is the general one (an agent that reads untrusted text, holds private data and can send requests out), and the mitigations are the familiar ones. The case for the product's rule that no agent input widens where it looks is a design argument from those sources, not an incident.
- **Not found at all:** a surveyed project that is both an agent tool and a network inventory with a person-set scope, a default rate, an identity string and a ledger. That gap is the product's opening, stated modestly below.

#### A reference point from the protocol's own reference servers

The reference fetch server ([README](https://github.com/modelcontextprotocol/servers/blob/main/src/fetch/README.md), the "servers" repository that note 27 counted at 92.1k stars) is not a network tool, but it is the nearest published example of a tool that takes an address from a model. By its README: it refuses hosts that resolve to private, loopback or link-local addresses, cloud-metadata endpoints included, by default; it checks every redirect the same way; an `--allow-private-ips` switch turns this off; an `--allowed-hosts` list must **also** be satisfied, so allow-listing an internal host alone does not open it; model-started fetches follow `robots.txt` and user-started ones do not; its default `User-Agent` names itself and labels a request "Autonomous" (the model) or "User-Specified" (the person). It states two gaps: DNS rebinding between the check and the connection, and names that only a proxy can resolve.

**What an agent tool that takes an address is expected to refuse**, by these sources taken together: an address it was not configured to reach (a fixed allow-list, not a deny-list); loopback, private, link-local, unique-local, multicast and cloud-metadata destinations unless the person has said otherwise; any scheme but the one the tool is for; a URL with embedded credentials; a redirect it did not check; a name that resolves to something other than what was checked; and, in any wrapper of an existing program, flags the model composes freely or text that reaches a shell.

## Part B: conduct

### 4. Looking at a network one is entitled to look at, and where it becomes hostile

**Norms of people who scan.** The ZMap project's [best practices](https://github.com/zmap/zmap/wiki/Scanning-Best-Practices) (page read) list six: identify yourself with reverse DNS entries, WHOIS records and a web page that describes the scans, with a way for operators to reach you; scan no more broadly or often than needed, at the lowest rate that meets the goal; offer an easy opt-out and list the ranges you scan from so that operators can block them; coordinate with the local IT and security team so scans do not overwhelm the local network; test new scanning code against your own systems and start small; and disclose the security problems you find, and tell owners of vulnerable systems. The ZMap [README](https://github.com/zmap/zmap) repeats the rate and opt-out rules. Masscan's README ([above](#a2-established-tools-that-are-not-for-agents)) gives a default of 100 packets per second and an exclude file. By search summary only: the same manners are repeated by a cloud provider's guidelines for network scanning, the Internet Census project, a Censys opt-out page, and a SANS Internet Storm Center ethics list (identification in reverse DNS and in an HTTP user agent, opt-out, abuse contacts, one transaction per second per destination service as one example, no denial of service). An IEEE Access study, as summarized in a search result, found that among scanners that could be identified about half had no effective opt-out; that is a reminder that these are norms, not enforcement.

**Where looking turns hostile.** Sources read directly give the tool-side line. Nmap's reference guide separates script categories and says some default scripts are intrusive and should not be run on networks without permission, and that the "all" selection includes exploit, brute-force and denial-of-service scripts. The agent wrappers above reflect the same line: one blocks exploit, dos and intrusive script categories, and one refuses to call high-risk tools "with no flag to override".

**Reconnaissance as defenders see it.** By search summary only (mirrors of the ATT&CK entry; the primary page was not read): network service discovery is a standard discovery technique, T1046; mitigations listed are disabling unneeded features, network intrusion prevention and network segmentation; detection looks for a process making many outbound connection attempts to administrative ports; and defenders are told to expect false alarms from legitimate asset-management and vulnerability scanners and to tune for them. The point for a product is that **its traffic will be seen and may be reported**. Also by search summary, NIST SP 800-115 (2008) carries a template for rules of engagement that fix what may be tested and when, before any testing starts; the PDF was not read.

**Law, at the level an engineer needs, and not legal advice.** All of the following is from search summaries; the primary texts and the Nmap legal chapter did not load.
- *United States.* A summary of the Nmap project's legal chapter says no US federal law explicitly makes port scanning a crime, but the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act turns on access "without authorization" and, in the provision quoted there, on damage; a civil case (Moulton v. VC3, 2000) held that the cost of investigating a port scan was not "damage"; many states have their own computer-abuse laws, some of which could arguably reach even a ping; and an Internet provider may end service for scanning under its terms. The Supreme Court's 2021 decision in *Van Buren* narrowed "exceeds authorized access", according to one commentary.
- *United Kingdom.* Commentary says whether scanning without consent is "unauthorised access" under the first offence in the Computer Misuse Act 1990 (maximum two years) has always been unclear; reform for security research has been proposed and was contested as of 2020 to 2023; the prosecution service's guidance exists but its treatment of scanning was not seen.
- *European Union.* Directive 2013/40/EU sets minimum offences for illegal access to, and interference with, information systems. Its recitals, by summary, mention that tools made for legitimate security testing are relevant to intent and that Member States should make it possible to detect and report security gaps legally.
- *Elsewhere* the summary reports a conviction in Finland for scanning a bank's ports. Other places were not looked at.
- The safe reading across all of it: **on a network the person owns or is entitled to administer, with the owner's knowledge, looking is ordinary administration; on anyone else's it is a legal risk that depends on place, intent and damage.** What makes a product's use legitimate is who set the scope.

**Acceptable-use rules.** By one search across universities (the pages were not read; the snippets are quoted by their summarizer): campus policies typically treat network mapping, port scanning or other security testing of systems one does not own or administer as off limits without prior written approval of a named security office; some extend the ban to packet capture and monitoring; they cite interference with network operations; staff may scan for compliance; and breaches go to conduct or discipline. A cloud provider and a campus wireless network are both examples of a network whose rules a person agrees to in order to use it. **Workplace, hosting and public wireless rules were not surveyed beyond this**, and no particular policy text was read.

### 5. What makes an inventory tool trusted by the people who run networks

None of the surveyed sources states this as one list. Read together, the sources above give these practices, each traced:

- **Says who it is.** ZMap's practices (reverse DNS, WHOIS, a page, contact), and, by search summary, a cloud provider's guidance for the HTTP user agent. The reference fetch server's default `User-Agent` names the server and marks a request as autonomous or user-specified. No surveyed agent network tool states how it identifies itself on the wire.
- **Slow by default.** ZMap ("slowest speed necessary"), Masscan (100 packets per second default, warnings), Nmap's template guidance (the default is the middle, and the author lists the others with reasons).
- **Stays in the range it was given.** The bug-bounty server's fail-closed allow list; the Pi-hole server's single named instance for writes; the MCP fetch server needing both an allow list and the private-address check. Nmap and Masscan offer exclusion lists for hosts that must not be touched.
- **Lets the owner opt out.** ZMap, Masscan and (search only) Censys and Internet Census.
- **Keeps a record of what it did.** No surveyed tool states one for what it sent. The Wireshark server writes only to named directories. NIST's rules-of-engagement template (search only) is a document written before testing. This is a gap in the field and a place for the product's ledger to be shown.
- **Never tries credentials, never exploits.** Nmap's guide puts brute-force, exploit and denial-of-service scripts apart from the rest and says the intrusive ones need permission. One wrapper blocks exploit, dos and intrusive categories; the MCP scanner never calls high and critical risk tools.
- **Stops when blocked.** Not stated as such in any source read; Nmap's own option set offers `--host-timeout` to give up on a target. The product's own rule (a block is a named stop) is stronger than anything found.
- **Reads what the network already knows first.** NetAlertX's imports from the router and the leases; NetBox's refusal to touch nodes.
- **Keeps what it finds on the machine.** NetAlertX's statement that data stays local unless notifications are configured.

### 6. Dual-use risk specific to agents

What was found, with a limit stated first: **no case, write-up or advisory was found in this survey that documents a real agent steered into scanning an internal range or reaching an internal service through a network tool.** A search for such material returned the general literature only. What exists, mostly by search summary:

- **The general frame.** The "lethal trifecta" (an agent that reads untrusted content, holds private data and can communicate outward) is the frame several sources use. The Frontier Model Forum's issue brief (summarized by a search result) proposes that an agent never hold all three at once; a Cloud Security Alliance note proposes splitting the roles across agents so that the one reading untrusted text has no power to act; both are reported as recommending an egress allow list so an injected instruction cannot send data to a new destination, confirmation before high-stakes or irreversible actions, and starting from minimal capability. Those pages are blogs and briefs, not standards; filtering by instruction ("be careful") is described as not enough.
- **OWASP's guidance on excessive agency** (LLM06 in the 2025 list; the page was not opened, summary only): give an agent only the tools it needs (a task that never fetches a page should have no URL-fetch tool); keep each tool narrow and avoid open-ended ones such as a shell; run each downstream action with the least permission, enforced by the downstream system and not trusted to the model; require a person's approval for high-impact actions; rate-limit; log and monitor.
- **SSRF in local servers.** One blog post (search only) records a maintainer closing a report of local SSRF as not applicable because the server runs locally, and argues that it can still reach whatever else listens on the machine and the cloud-metadata address. The protocol's own reference fetch server decided the other way and refuses private ranges by default.
- **What the surveyed tools themselves admit.** The README of one of the surveyed tool sets calls its read-only switch a guardrail and not a permission boundary (DBHub, in note 27); the MCP guard scanner publishes its rebinding gap; the older Tailscale server says its read-only claim is "in theory".
- **Mitigations the sources recommend, collected:** scopes fixed by a person (the bug-bounty server's allow list, in a file the model does not write); no free-form targets or flags (the one-flag wrapper is the counter-example); confirmation for new destinations (the sandbox runtime's callback, Claude Code's first-use prompt as summarized, Chrome's permission prompt); read-only defaults (AWS, the UniFi Site Manager, the Pi-hole read-only set); and logging (OWASP, summary only).

### 7. Privacy

- **What a list of devices reveals.** By search summary of the studies: a 2013 study of a university network's announced device names found names that carry people's identity, set automatically at first setup; a security-company post reports that some operating systems by default build hostnames from the owner's name and announce them on the link; a 2018 analysis of home IoT traffic tracked one tablet across eight randomized MAC addresses by joining the DHCP and DNS logs through its host name; a study of 93 smart-home devices (IMC 2023, a poster at NDSS 2024) found MAC addresses, device names and UUIDs in plaintext in local traffic, usable to fingerprint a household, and found apps and SDKs collecting them. An IETF discussion thread proposes sending device names only to paired hosts. From these, a device list shows who lives or works there, what they own, when they are home, and what in the home is a medical, security or personal device.
- **How careful products handle it.** One product read states its stance: NetAlertX keeps its data on the machine and sends nothing out unless notifications are configured. The UniFi servers redact known secret fields by default; the LibreNMS server redacts SNMP credentials. **No product read here redacts device names or addresses in logs, and none states a policy on device names.** The ones reading from a router (NetAlertX, the controller servers) inherit the router's own list and do not add to what is learned.

## What a careful design would take from this

These are inputs, not decisions. Another agent designs. Each is traced.

1. **A scope that a person writes and an agent cannot change, with fail-closed behavior when it is empty.** Source: the bug-bounty server's `ALLOWED_TARGETS`; the sandbox runtime's empty list meaning none. Matches [section 502](../spec/5-security/502-defending-against-a-manipulated-agent.md)'s protected settings.
2. **The network's own boundary before the person's scope.** Loopback, link-local, multicast, unspecified and cloud-metadata destinations are not targets of an inventory by default and need a named reason; private ranges are the interesting case, because the sandbox runtime leaves them open for stated reasons and the web-security sources block them for servers. For a tool whose purpose is the local network, private ranges are in scope only inside what the person said. Sources: sandbox runtime, OWASP, the fetch server.
3. **No free-form target or flag from an agent.** An agent names a thing from what the product already found or from a set the person fixed, not an address string it composed; no pass-through of tool flags. Sources: the one-flag nmap wrapper as the counter-example; OWASP's "accept a host, not a URL"; the MCP guard scanner's rule about unrestricted address arguments.
4. **Resolve once, check, connect to what was checked.** Normalize IPv6-mapped and encoded forms first; do not follow redirects off the declared surface. Sources: OWASP; the sandbox runtime; the fetch server and MCP guard scanner (which state their gaps).
5. **A default pace slow enough to be unremarkable, a stated ceiling, and a cap on how many hosts one request may touch.** Sources: ZMap and Masscan; the 256-target cap and 600-second ceiling in one wrapper; LibreNMS' optional rate limit. The numbers are the design's to choose and to measure.
6. **Start from what the network already holds, and look on the local link before anything routed.** Router and lease lists, name announcements, ARP, and only then probes, and only for hosts inside scope. Source: NetAlertX's imports; NetBox's separation; Nmap's use of ARP on a local segment.
7. **Say who is looking.** A stable, truthful identity string wherever the protocol has a field for one; a way for a network operator to find out what it was and why. Source: ZMap's identification practice; the fetch server's labeled user agent.
8. **Never try credentials, never exploit, never use an intrusive probe without the person naming it.** Keep these classes separate in the vocabulary, as Nmap separates its script categories. Sources: Nmap; the two wrappers that block categories. This agrees with rule 5 and rule 7.
9. **Keep the record the field does not keep.** What was sent, to where, when, at whose instruction. Source: the gap (no surveyed agent tool states a record); NIST's rules-of-engagement template, search only.
10. **Reads and probes are different classes.** A reading of the machine's own tables is not a probe of another host. A tool that writes to a device or controller is a third class again, with preview and confirmation by default. Sources: the UniFi and Pi-hole servers; the AWS server's read-only claim; the Wireshark servers' marking of reads and writes.
11. **Reading raw traffic is its own class.** A capture shows other people's data. The Wireshark servers cap capture length and packet count, restrict output folders, and in one case drop capture altogether in a profile. Sources: both Wireshark servers.
12. **Treat device names as personal data from the first read.** Keep them local, mark them untrusted text (also an injection route, per section 502), and have a redaction stance for logs. Sources: the device-name and smart-home studies; NetAlertX's local-only statement; the absence of any product that redacts.
13. **A person is asked in words they can understand when a destination is new.** Sources: the sandbox runtime's callback; Claude Code's first-use prompt (summary); Chrome's permission text.
14. **Say plainly what the scope check cannot do.** The strongest sources publish their gaps (rebinding, proxy-resolved names, NAT). Source: the sandbox runtime, the fetch server, the MCP guard scanner, DBHub (note 27).
15. **Treat the person's entitlement as the person's statement, and keep it narrow.** The surveyed tools put authorization on the operator ("authorization remains the operator's responsibility") and the law and campus rules turn on who owns or may administer a network. A statement tied to a named network, with the product refusing others, is easier to defend than a general permission. Sources: the bug-bounty server; the university policies (search only); the legal summaries (search only).

## Where this product would differ from what exists, stated modestly

- The surveyed agent tools state their authorization as advice and enforce little or nothing. This product's design (decision 0222, rule 5, section 502) would make scope a thing the person sets, the policy gate decides, and the agent cannot widen. One surveyed project has this shape; it is small and its use is unknown.
- The surveyed tools mostly let a model supply the target. The design would let an agent choose from what the scout has found within a person's scope; nothing read here does that. It has not been built or tried.
- No surveyed agent tool states a record of its own network activity. The ledger would be a record the person and a network's administrator can ask for. Whether that is valued by administrators is not known from these sources.
- The established scanners are pointed by a person and have no agent in the loop; the design is of a different kind of tool and the nearest norms (ZMap, Masscan, Nmap) were written for a human operator. Whether their manners suit a tool that may be asked many times by an agent is the design's question.
- A website reached across networks, as decision 0222 puts it, is one case of the network; the surveyed tools treat the web (fetch) and the local network (scan) as separate worlds with different defaults, and the fetch server's defaults are the strict ones. Putting them under one idea is something none of the surveyed projects does.
